# Polar-product optimizer step-time profile — initial measurement

**Date:** 2026-05-04. **Hardware:** RTX A6000 (NOT canonical — A100 was unavailable due to QOSMaxGRESPerUser limit; numbers are relative-only and do not transfer to A100). **Single seed.**

## Why this doc

`docs/notes/polar_product/algorithm.md` Algorithm 1 is converging on production. Before optimizing wall time we need to know **where time goes** inside `AdamPolarProductLoRA.step()`. This is the first measurement; figures are for relative comparison across components on this card and should be re-taken on A100 before any speedup target gets committed to a sweep budget.

## Setup

- **Script:** `scripts/bench_polar_product_components.py`
- **Output JSONL:** `logs/profile_polar_components/a6000_initial.jsonl`
- **Instrumentation:** `lora_playground/_step_timer.py` (`CudaTimer`) + `maybe_time(timer, name)` wraps in `optim.py:_polar_pipeline` and `AdamPolarProductLoRA.step`. Default-off when `optimizer._step_timer is None`; all 48 polar-product unit tests pass with the timer wired.
- **Model:** `allenai/OLMo-2-0425-1B`, `target_modules=all-linear`, 112 LoRA pairs, bf16.
- **Per cell:** `n_warmup=3`, `n_cycles=4`, `grad_accum_steps=8`, `batch_size=2`, `seq_len=512`, `lr=1e-3`. Refresh vs stale steps are split by swapping `optimizer._step_timer` between two `CudaTimer` instances based on `pair_state[0]['step'] % K`.
- **Reproduce:**
  ```
  python scripts/bench_polar_product_components.py --bf16 \
      --lora_r 16 64 \
      --optimizers adamw adam-polar-product-lora adam-polar-product-lora-coupled \
      --precond_refresh_every 1 16 \
      --n_warmup 3 --n_cycles 4 \
      --out logs/profile_polar_components/a6000_initial.jsonl
  ```

`outer_step_ms` is `optimizer.step()` only (forward/backward not counted). Per-component figures use the lazy CUDA-event pattern: events are recorded inline; `elapsed_time` is only resolved at end-of-cell after a single `synchronize`. Coverage % = `Σ leaves / outer_step_ms`; uncovered remainder is Python overhead and inter-scope kernel-launch gaps.

## Observations

- **`precond_refresh` dominates the refresh-step cost at r=64.** 220 ms = 57 % of k=1 refresh-step total (32 % at k=3). At r=16 it's 80 ms (33 % / 16 %). The raw eigh per (r×r) SPD matrix is small; the cost is plausibly the per-pair Python loop launching 2 × 112 = 224 small `eigh` kernels per refresh step. Raising `precond_refresh_every` is the existing lever — at K=16 the stale-step cost drops by 90 ms (r=16, k=1) and 240 ms (r=64, k=1), confirming the cache pays off and the eigh storm is the cost. The `higham` precond-method exists as a Newton-Schulz-based alternative; not measured here.
- **Newton-Schulz polar (`polar_NS_A` + `polar_NS_B`) is the second-largest cost.** ~75 ms total per step at k=1 (35 % of stale, both r); ~225 ms at k=3 (~52 % of stale, both r). Notably this scales weakly with r (37→41 ms going r=16→64) — the cost is launch-bound across 112 pairs more than per-matrix work. The two halves are near-symmetric.
- **`picard_cross_coupling` is free at k=1 and material at k=3.** ~1.5 ms (just assignments) vs ~53 ms (r=16) / 70 ms (r=64) at k=3. The k=3 cost is two matmuls per Picard iter (`B^T @ dB_prev @ A`, `B @ dA_prev @ A^T`) running over 2 correction iters × 112 pairs.
- **`polar_unwhiten_rescale` is heavier than I expected.** 18–22 ms at k=1, 57–69 ms at k=3. The block does the `S^{-1/2} P` unwhitening, the Muon+ norm, and the Frobenius rescale — three matmul/norm passes per pair per Picard iter. Candidate for fusing.
- **`adam_direction` is r-independent** at ~17 ms — it's per-coord on (gA, gB), so cost is dominated by the LoRA-param count (constant across r=16 vs r=64 since `d_out` × r vs `d_out` × 16 is the variable; A: r×d_in, B: d_out×r — and d_in, d_out, n_pairs are fixed). The constant offset is plausibly kernel-launch overhead from per-tensor in-place ops.
- **`apply` is negligible** (~5 ms regardless).
- **AdamW r=64 is 5.3 ms** — sets the floor any LoRA-side speedup must respect. The polar-product overhead vs AdamW per step is dominated by `precond_refresh` (when refreshing) and the 112-pair NS launches (always).

## Overhead view (step − AdamW-equivalent)

`adam_direction` (per-coord Adam on `gA`, `gB`) and `apply` (the `add_` calls) are the AdamW-equivalent baseline that any LoRA optimizer pays. Stripping them isolates the polar-product **overhead**, which is what speedup work has to attack.

The AdamW-equivalent baseline is essentially constant across variants (~22 ms per step on this card; the LoRA-param count is fixed). The polar overhead is what changes:

| adam-polar-product-lora | 16 | 1 | refresh | 22.4 | 180.1 | 8.0× |
| adam-polar-product-lora | 16 | 16 | stale | 20.6 | 98.1 | 4.8× |
| adam-polar-product-lora | 64 | 1 | refresh | 22.6 | 338.7 | 15.0× |
| adam-polar-product-lora | 64 | 16 | stale | 21.2 | 106.3 | 5.0× |

### Read

- **Newton–Schulz polar is the dominant amortized overhead.** On stale steps (the common case under K=16 caching), `polar_NS_A + NS_B` is **60–75 %** of polar-specific overhead across every (r, k) combination. The total NS time is ~75 ms at k=1 / ~225 ms at k=3 and scales only weakly with r — symptom of launch-bound execution across 112 pairs, not per-matrix work. **This is the speedup target with broadest payoff.**
- **`precond_refresh` dominates *refresh* steps at r=64** (67 % of overhead at k=1) — but refresh fires on 1 / K of steps, so its amortized share at K=16 is ~4 %. The refresh share is what currently sets the K floor (cache further → eval-loss risk, not measured here); attacking eigh latency directly is a path to lowering the floor, not the dominant amortized cost.
- **`polar_unwhiten_rescale` is a consistent ~17–20 % of stale overhead.** Three matmul/norm passes per pair per Picard iter; second-rank target.
- **`picard_cross_coupling` is k=3-only.** ~16–18 % of stale overhead at k=3, ~1 % at k=1. Two matmuls per Picard correction iter, per pair.
- **`polar_whiten`** is consistently small (3–5 % of stale overhead) — single matmul per pair per side, not a target.
- **`adamw_equivalent` is r-flat at ~22 ms.** The polar variants don't pay extra for `adam_direction` or `apply`; the overhead really is in the polar pipeline.

The amortized story: **batch the per-pair Newton–Schulz across pairs**. That single change attacks ~60–75 % of every polar variant's stale-step overhead, on every (r, k) we care about.

## Where to look for wall-time wins

Four candidates by descending impact, none of them executed yet:

1. **Batch the per-pair work across pairs.** The 112-way Python loop is the largest theme — `polar_NS_{A,B}`, `precond_refresh`, `polar_unwhiten_rescale`, and the k=3 `picard_cross_coupling` are all per-pair. If LoRA pairs share `(d_out, r)` shapes within a model (they do, by transformer block), they stack into 3-D tensors and the inner ops become single batched matmul / batched eigh / batched NS. The kernel-launch storm should compress into 1–4 launches per scope.
2. **Cheaper / more-cached preconditioners.** `precond_refresh_every=K=16` already saves ~90–240 ms / step on stale steps; whether K can be raised further without eval-loss cost is an empirical question (not addressed here). Switching `precond_method=higham` is a one-line knob and worth a single measurement at r=64.
3. **Fewer NS iterations.** Default `j=5` (Algorithm 0). At quintic convergence, `j=3` may already saturate every singular value to within working precision on the matrices arising here. One sweep would tell us.
4. **Fuse `polar_unwhiten_rescale`.** Combining the unwhitening matmul, the Muon+ norm, and the Frobenius rescale into a single fused op would shave the 18–69 ms component but probably needs custom kernels.

These are recorded as candidates. Picking one to attack is the next plan.

## Update 2026-05-04: batched-NS microbenchmark (Phase B of speedup work)

The "batch NS across pairs" candidate from the table above was tested in isolation against the per-pair Python loop, *without* touching the optimizer. Implementation and bench:

- `lora_playground.optim._newton_schulz_batched` — drop-in batched NS for `X.shape == (..., m, n)`. Same Muon orthogonalization (per-matrix Frobenius pre-norm, no post-multiply).
- `scripts/bench_ns_batched.py` — equivalence + timing on the six real shape groups for OLMo-2-1B at r=16, j=5.
- `tests/test_newton_schulz_batched.py` — 24 regression tests (parametrized over 7 shapes × 3 j-values + dtype, broadcasting, orthogonality property).

Reproduce: `python scripts/bench_ns_batched.py`.

| group | N | shape | loop ms | batched ms | speedup |
|---|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A_64×16×2048 | 64 | small | 18.0 | 0.7 | **24×** |
| B_64×2048×16 | 64 | small | 19.0 | 2.3 | 8.2× |
| A_32×16×2048 | 32 | small | 8.9 | 0.4 | 22× |
| B_32×8192×16 | 32 | large | 10.3 | 6.7 | 1.5× |
| A_16×16×8192 | 16 | medium | 4.9 | 0.8 | 5.9× |
| B_16×2048×16 | 16 | medium | 4.8 | 1.5 | 3.2× |
| **total (sum of medians)** | | | **65.9** | **12.5** | **5.3×** |

Equivalence: max-abs-err ≈ 8.2e-8, max-rel-err ≈ 1.4e-6 vs per-matrix `_newton_schulz` across all groups at j=5 (well within fp32 noise). The 72-test pytest suite (existing polar tests + new batched-NS tests) passes with the helper added.

The pattern matches the launch-bound hypothesis exactly: largest speedups on shape groups with many small matrices (24× and 22× on the 64- and 32-pair small groups, where `_newton_schulz` was launching ~3 kernels per matrix per iter × 5 iters × 64 matrices = 960 launches per call); smallest speedup (1.5×) on the one group of 32 large matrices where per-call work was already non-negligible.

**Projection back onto whole-step times** (NS_A + NS_B reduction, A6000):

| config | NS now | NS batched (proj) | savings | step now | step proj |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| r=16, k=1, refresh | 77 | 14 | 63 | 232 | 169 |
| r=16, k=1, stale (K=16) | 73 | 14 | 59 | 142 | 83 |
| r=16, k=3, refresh | 223 | 42 | 181 | 504 | 323 |
| r=16, k=3, stale (K=16) | 217 | 41 | 176 | 413 | 237 |

(Projection assumes the same 5.3× holds when the batched call is invoked from inside the optimizer; integration may surface secondary costs not visible in isolation. K=16 numbers are reference; production currently runs K=1.)

This is a primitive-level win. The full payoff requires integrating the batched call into `AdamPolarProductLoRA.step()`, which means refactoring the per-pair loop to per-shape-group. That refactor isn't done yet — separate PR.

## Caveats

- **Hardware mismatch.** A6000 ≠ A100. Bandwidth, clocks, and SM count differ enough that wall-time ranks across components may shuffle on A100. Re-measure before acting on a number.
- **Single seed, single run per cell.** Variance is not characterized — `n_cycles=4` gives `n_steps_phase ∈ {4, 60}` per phase per cell. Refresh-step cells with `n_cycles=4` see only 4 refresh steps each; the means are correspondingly noisy.
- **`outer_step_ms` excludes fwd/bwd.** Real wall-time-per-training-step also pays the forward + backward; the optimizer-step share within total is smaller than the numbers above suggest. `scripts/bench_optimizer_step.py` reports the inclusive number.
- **Harness overhead.** Forward+backward (8 grad-accum × 512 seq) at this batch size dominates the bench wall time even though it's not what we measure. For follow-up runs, dropping `grad_accum_steps=1 --seq_len 128` cuts bench wall time without affecting the optimizer-internal numbers (the optimizer's cost depends on LoRA shapes, not on gradient values).
