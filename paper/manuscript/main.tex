Deployment of large-scale pretrained models increasingly depends on adapting a
general model to many specialized tasks~\cite{foundation_models}. Finetuning a separate full copy for each task is expensive, because training must hold gradients and optimizer state for the whole model, and deploying each task means storing and serving multiple full-sized models. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA~\cite{lora}) avoids both issues by freezing the base weight $W_0 \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{out}} \times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ and adding a small low-rank update,
\begin{equation}
W = W_0 + \DW, \qquad \DW = \frac{\alpha}{r}\,BA, \qquad
B \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}} \times r}, \quad
A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_{\mathrm{in}}},
\label{eq:lora}
\end{equation}
to each layer. With only the adapter trained, finetuning can run on nearly the same hardware layouts as inference, and the shared base lets one server serve many adapters at once~\cite{punica,bitdelta,schulman2025lora}.


The standard optimization recipe for LoRA applies Adam~\cite{adam} to the two factors $A$ and $B$, treating all the parameters as a single flat vector and ignoring any matrix structure. In language-model pretraining, matrix-aware optimizers such as Muon~\cite{muon} and Shampoo~\cite{shampoo} treat each weight as a matrix and outperform Adam~\cite{liu2025muon,shampoo,benchmark_practices,optimizer_benchmarking,qiu2026hyperparameter}, so it is natural to ask whether the same gains carry over to LoRA. 

However, naively applying a matrix-aware optimizer to each factor does not improve on Adam. The model sees the \emph{product} $BA$, while the optimizer acts on the \emph{factors} $A$ and $B$ individually. Several concurrent works try to address this by proposing updates that are variants of a product-aware~\cite{imuon,loramuon,tilde_csd} Muon step we refer to as \emph{Product Muon} (\Cref{sec:product-muon}). In our experiments, we find that the Product Muon step is also unable to consistently improve over Adam.

This motivates \methodname{}, which is derived from a novel formulation motivated by controlling the per-sample loss change across a batch. In the terminology of the community working on variant of Muon, \methodname{} is LMO (Linear Minimization Oracle) based method, with a new constraint on the change in the per-sample loss. This formulation gives as a solution a curvature-preconditioned update in the spectral norm. We then develop a product-aware version that can be applied to LoRA. Additionally, unlike the Product Muon step, we control the factor update sizes as well as the size of the update to the product.

We evaluate \methodname{} through an extensive numerical study spanning multiple base models, datasets, adapter ranks, and learning-rate settings. As previewed in \Cref{fig:hero}, \methodname{} consistently improves training efficiency over Adam and matrix-aware LoRA baselines, reaching Adam's final held-out loss in $1.2$--$1.7\times$ fewer steps across our different settings, with only a less than 3\% cost increase of each step as compared to Adam. We complement these comparisons with component ablations that separate the effects of the curvature-aware direction and magnitude controls we introduce, showing that both contribute to the overall speedup of \methodname{} over our baselines.
 Finally, we find that \methodname{} is  easier to tune than Adam: its optimal learning rate is stable across rank, and performance is less sensitive to the learning rate.

\section{\texorpdfstring{\methodname{}}{PoLoRA}: Preconditioned Orthogonalized LoRA}



\label{sec:method}
To build up to the \methodname{} update, in \Cref{sec:muon} we first recall the derivation of Muon from the linear minimization oracle (LMO) perspective. 
This perspective views the Muon update as minimizing the linearized loss subject to controlling the spectral norm of the update. Next, in \Cref{sec:product-muon} we show how this LMO can be made product-aware, yielding the Product Muon LMO. 
In \Cref{sec:sample-loss-lmo} we derive a new base LMO that bounds the change in the per-sample loss rather than the spectral norm of the update. We then derive its product-aware version for LoRA. The LMO provides the update directions, but in order to ensure the sizes of the per-factor updates are well-behaved we rescale the updates to simultaneously control the per-factor update and merged-weight update sizes.

\subsection{Muon}\label{sec:muon}
For a weight $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}} \times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ with gradient $G = \nabla_W \mathcal{L}(W)$, the Muon LMO~\cite{bernstein2025modular,carlson2015preconditioned} minimizes the local linearization of the loss subject to a ball constraint,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{array}{ll}
  \underset{\Delta W}{\mathrm{minimize}} &
  \ip{G, \Delta W} \\[2pt]
  \mbox{subject to} &
  \displaystyle
  \norm{\Delta W}_2
  \le \tau,
  \end{array}
  \label{eq:lmo}
\end{equation}
where $\|\cdot\|_2$ is the spectral norm 
\[
\|W\|_2 := \max_{\|x\|_2 =1} \|W x \|_2.
\]
Bounding the spectral norm controls the largest amount by which the update can change the layer's output over all unit-norm inputs~\cite{yang2023spectral}.
With this choice, the LMO direction is the matrix sign of the gradient~\cite{bernstein2025modular}
\[
    \Delta W = -\tau \msign(G),
\]
where $\msign(G)=UV^\top$ for the reduced SVD $G=U\Sigma V^\top$ (\Cref{lem:muon-lmo}). In practice, Muon sets $\tau$ to be the learning rate $\eta$, applies the matrix sign to a momentum buffer rather than the raw gradient $G$, and approximates the matrix sign operation using Newton--Schulz iterations (\Cref{app:gramns}). Next we show how to adapt this LMO to be aware of the product structure in LoRA~\eqref{eq:lora}.

\subsection{Product Muon}\label{sec:product-muon}
Consider a LoRA step that updates the two factors $A$ and $B$ as follows
\[
A \leftarrow A + \Delta A,
\qquad
B \leftarrow B + \Delta B.
\]
Applying Muon naively would impose a spectral bound separately on $\Delta A$ and $\Delta B$, but what matters to the layer is the effective update of the product $BA$ which is not controlled solely by $\Delta A$ and $\Delta B$. Indeed, the update to the merged weight is to first-order,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:DeltaW-lin}
\Delta W = (B + \Delta B)(A + \Delta A) - BA \approx B \Delta A + \Delta B A,
\end{equation}
from which we see that the spectral norm bound should be applied to this linearized product update. Substituting the above~\eqref{eq:DeltaW-lin} into the Muon LMO~\eqref{eq:lmo} gives the spectral constraint
\[
\norm{B \Delta A + \Delta B A}_2
\le \tau.
\]
The constraint couples the two unknowns $\Delta A$ and $\Delta B$, but we can decouple them with the upper bound from norm subadditivity
\[
\norm{B \Delta A + \Delta B A}_2
\le
\norm{B \Delta A}_2 + \norm{\Delta B A}_2.
\]
As a result, we get the \emph{Product Muon} LMO
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \Delta A &\in \argmin_{\Delta A}~ \ip{G_A, \Delta A}
  \quad \textnormal{subject to}\quad
  \norm{B \Delta A}_2\le\tau, \\
  \Delta B &\in \argmin_{\Delta B}~ \ip{G_B, \Delta B}
  \quad \textnormal{subject to}\quad
  \norm{\Delta B A}_2\le\tau,
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:decoupled-lora-lmo}
\end{equation}
where the factor gradients are
\begin{equation}\label{e-grad-wrt-factors}
    G_A := \nabla_A \mathcal{L}(W_0+BA) = B^\top G,
    \qquad
    G_B := \nabla_B \mathcal{L}(W_0+BA) = G A^\top.
\end{equation}

The solution to this LMO is given by
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \Delta A &= -\tau\,(B^\top B)^{-1/2}\,\msign\!\big((B^\top B)^{-1/2} G_A\big), \\
    \Delta B &= -\tau\,\msign\!\big(G_B (A A^\top)^{-1/2}\big)\,(A A^\top)^{-1/2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Similar to Muon, these factor updates are computed using momentum buffers and with $\tau = \eta$.

\subsection{Per-Sample Loss Control}\label{sec:sample-loss-lmo}
We now derive the base LMO that will be the starting point of \methodname{}. Let $\{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$ be a batch of input activations to the layer that $W$ encodes, and let $G_i$ be the gradient associated with $x_i$, that is, with a slight abuse of notation $G_i = \nabla \ell_{x_i}(W)$, where the abuse comes from using $\ell_{x_i}$ to denote the loss with respect to this activation rather than a full input.

To be conservative with respect to every activation, we enforce that no single activation results in a large change in the loss, that is we want
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss-diff}
     \ell_{x_i}(W +\Delta W) - \ell_{x_i} (W)\leq \tau, \qquad  i=1,\ldots, n.
\end{equation} 
This would ensure stable training with no spikes or jumps for the current batch loss.  
But the loss is highly non-linear and non-convex, so instead we use the local linearization of the loss given by
\[  
\ell_{x_i}(W +\Delta W) -\ell_{x_i} (W)  \approx \ip{G_i, \Delta W}. 
\]
Replacing the spectral-norm constraint in~\eqref{eq:lmo} with the per-sample bounds~\eqref{eq:loss-diff}, linearized as above, gives
\begin{equation}
 \begin{array}{ll}
  \underset{\Delta W}{\mathrm{minimize}} &
  \ip{G, \Delta W} \\
  \mbox{subject to} &
  \displaystyle  
\ip{G_i, \Delta W} 
  \le \tau, \qquad i=1,\ldots, n.
  \end{array}
  \label{eq:sample-lmo}
\end{equation}
Since we do not have efficient direct access to the per-input gradients $G_i$, we will now define a \emph{outergradient} set $\mathcal{G}$ such that $G_i \in \mathcal{G}$,
use the following inequality
\begin{equation} \label{eq:outgrad1}
    \max_{i=1,\ldots, n}  \ip{G_i, \Delta W}  
    \leq   \max_{\tilde G \in \mathcal{G}} \; \ip{\tilde G, \Delta W},
\end{equation} 
and then impose  that the right hand side of \eqref{eq:outgrad1} 
is less than $\tau$. 
To build $\mathcal{G}$ we collect some standard properties of the gradient matrices in the following lemma.
\begin{restatable}[Properties of gradients]{lemma}{gradprop} 
  \label{lem:gradprop}
    Let $g_i=\vec(G_i) \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{out}} d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ for all $i=1, \ldots, n$,
    and $\Sigma := \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n g_i g_i^\top$. It follows that the gradients satisfy
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item {\bf Leverage bound:} $ \ip{\Sigma^\dagger g_i, g_i} \leq n$, \\[-0.5cm]
        \item {\bf Rank one:} $G_i = b_i x_i^\top$,
        \end{enumerate}
    for some $x_i \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ and $b_i \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}$.
\end{restatable}

The above properties suggest the following outergradient set
\begin{equation} 
\label{eq:outgrad} 
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma) := \left \{ \tilde G = b x^\top ~\bigg |~  
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}},
    \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}, \
\ip{\Sigma^\dagger \vec(\tilde G), \vec(\tilde G)} \leq n \right \}.
\end{equation}
By~\Cref{lem:gradprop} we have that $G_i \in \mathcal{G}$, thus we can use this $\mathcal{G}$ in our upper bound~\eqref{eq:outgrad1}.
The last remaining issue is that $\Sigma$ is too large of a matrix to store or use directly. To address this issue we replace $\Sigma$ with a structured approximation.

\paragraph{Kronecker approximation.}
Following the Kronecker-factored approximations used by Shampoo~\cite{shampoo, klshampoo}
and KFAC~\cite{kfac}, let $P\in \R^{d_{\mathrm{out}} \times d_{\mathrm{out}}}$ and $Q  \in\R^{d_{\mathrm{in}} \times d_{\mathrm{in}}} $ be symmetric positive definite matrices such that
     \begin{equation} \label{eq:pq-factor}
     \Sigma  \approx Q \otimes P.\end{equation}
     Using the factorization above, if we use our outergradient set~\eqref{eq:outgrad} to relax the constraint in~\eqref{eq:sample-lmo} through~\eqref{eq:outgrad1}, then we arrive at the following LMO
\begin{equation}
 \begin{array}{ll}
  \underset{\Delta W}{\mathrm{minimize}} &
  \ip{G, \Delta W} \\[2pt]
\mbox{subject to} &
  \displaystyle  
\max_{\tilde G \in \mathcal{G}(Q \otimes P)} \ip{\tilde G, \Delta W} 
  \le \tau.
  \end{array}
  \label{eq:polorafull-hard}
\end{equation}
Fortunately the above can be simplified. By~\Cref{lem:pseudogradient-spectral} the constraint in~\eqref{eq:polorafull-hard} can be re-written using the identity 
\[ 
\max_{G \in \mathcal{G}(Q \otimes P)}\ip{G, \Delta W}  
= \sqrt{n} \| P^{1/2} \Delta W Q^{1/2}\|_2.
\]
Then \eqref{eq:polorafull-hard} can be rewritten as
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{ll}
\underset{\Delta W}{\mathrm{minimize}} &
\ip{G, \Delta W} \\[2pt]
\mathrm{subject~ to} &
\displaystyle  
\|P^{1/2} \Delta W Q^{1/2}\|_2 \leq  \tau,
\end{array}
\label{eq:polorafull}
\end{equation}
where we have absorbed a $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ into $\tau$, that is $\tau \leftarrow \frac{\tau}{\sqrt{n}}$.
The LMO in~\eqref{eq:polorafull} is an instance of the following preconditioned spectral-norm LMO.
\begin{restatable}[Preconditioned spectral-norm LMO]{lemma}{GeneralLmoUpdate}
\label{lem:whitened-spectral-lmo}
Let $L$ and $R$ be symmetric positive definite. Then
\begin{equation}\label{e-gen-spectral-lmo-update}
X^\star = -\tau L^{-1}
\msign\left(L^{-1}MR^{-1}\right) R^{-1}
\end{equation}
is a solution of
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\underset{\|LXR\|_2\leq\tau}{\mathrm{minimize}}&
\ip{M,X}.
\end{array}
\]
Moreover, $X^\star$ is the unique minimizer of
$\|LXR\|_F$ over the solution set.
\end{restatable}
Applying the lemma above with $(L,R)=(P^{1/2},Q^{1/2})$, a solution to \eqref{eq:polorafull} is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polorafull-update}
    \Delta W =  - \tau P^{-1/2} \msign(P^{-1/2} G Q^{-1/2}) Q^{-1/2}.
\end{equation} 


\paragraph{Direction.}
So far we have described an update for the full weight matrix $W \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{out}} \times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$. We now adapt this update to LoRA~\eqref{eq:lora}, computing its \emph{direction} and its \emph{magnitude} separately. 
To set the direction, we adapt the preconditioned spectral-norm
  LMO~\eqref{eq:polorafull}. The magnitude is set separately below.
  
 
Replacing $\Delta W$ in \eqref{eq:polorafull} with its linearized product update~\eqref{eq:DeltaW-lin} gives the LMO
\begin{equation}
    \begin{array}{ll}
    \underset{\Delta A,\Delta B}{\mathrm{minimize}} &
    \ip{G, B\Delta A+\Delta B A} \\[2pt]
    \mbox{subject to} &
    \norm{P^{1/2}(B\Delta A+\Delta B A)Q^{1/2}}_2
    \le \tau.
    \end{array}
    \label{eq:joint-direction-lmo}
  \end{equation}
  Since we will set the magnitude later, the reader can ignore the exact value of $\tau$.
The constraint in \eqref{eq:joint-direction-lmo} couples $\Delta A$ and $\Delta B$ inside a single spectral norm. Subadditivity of the spectral norm bounds it by the two factor steps separately,
\[
\norm{P^{1/2}(B\Delta A+\Delta B A)Q^{1/2}}_2
\leq \norm{P^{1/2}B\Delta A Q^{1/2}}_2
+ \norm{P^{1/2} \Delta B A Q^{1/2}}_2,
\]
so bounding each factor step bounds the merged update. Using the above upper bound in~\eqref{eq:joint-direction-lmo}
% We therefore impose one spectral-norm constraint per factor,
% \[
% \norm{P^{1/2}B\Delta A Q^{1/2}}_2 \le \tau_A,
% \qquad
% \norm{P^{1/2}\Delta B A Q^{1/2}}_2 \le \tau_B ,
% \]
 decouples the product LMO into two factorwise LMOs.
That is, using factor gradients~\eqref{e-grad-wrt-factors},
the LMO~\eqref{eq:joint-direction-lmo} decouples into two factorwise LMOs
\begin{equation}
\setlength{\arraycolsep}{2pt}
\begin{array}{@{}c@{\qquad}c@{}}
\begin{array}{ll}
\underset{\Delta A}{\mathrm{minimize}} &
\ip{G_A,\Delta A} \\[2pt]
\mbox{subject to} &
\norm{P^{1/2}B\Delta A Q^{1/2}}_2 \le \tau ,
\end{array}
&
\begin{array}{ll}
\underset{\Delta B}{\mathrm{minimize}} &
\ip{G_B,\Delta B} \\[2pt]
\mbox{subject to} &
\norm{P^{1/2}\Delta B A Q^{1/2}}_2 \le \tau .
\end{array}
\end{array}
\label{eq:decoupled-direction-lmo}
\end{equation}
Define the $r\times r$ matrices
\begin{equation}
C_A = B^\top P B,
\qquad
C_B = A Q A^\top .
\label{eq:ours-closure}
\end{equation}
By definition of the spectral norm, and using the above definitions of $C_A$ and $C_B$,  the left hand side of the two constraints in \eqref{eq:decoupled-direction-lmo} can be re-written as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:collapse-constraint}
\norm{P^{1/2}B\Delta A Q^{1/2}}_2=\norm{C_A^{1/2}\Delta A Q^{1/2}}_2,
\qquad
\norm{P^{1/2}\Delta B A Q^{1/2}}_2=\norm{P^{1/2}\Delta B C_B^{1/2}}_2 .
\end{equation}
Using~\eqref{eq:collapse-constraint} in~\eqref{eq:decoupled-direction-lmo}, each factorwise LMO is an instance of \Cref{lem:whitened-spectral-lmo}, with $(L,R)=(C_A^{1/2},Q^{1/2})$ for the $\Delta A$ update and $(L,R)=(P^{1/2},C_B^{1/2})$ for the $\Delta B$ update. Thus the two minimizers are $-\tau D_A$ and $-\tau D_B$, where
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  D_A &=
  C_A^{-1/2}\,
  \msign\!\left(C_A^{-1/2}\,G_A\,Q^{-1/2}\right)
  \,Q^{-1/2},\\
  D_B &=
  P^{-1/2}\,
  \msign\!\left(P^{-1/2}\,G_B\,C_B^{-1/2}\right)
  \,C_B^{-1/2}.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:factor-directions}
\end{equation}
The minimizers scale linearly with the budgets, $\Delta A=-\tau D_A$ and $\Delta B=-\tau D_B$, so $\tau$ controls the magnitude while $D_A$ and $D_B$ give the factor update directions.
% We therefore leave the budgets free and set the step magnitude directly through the learning rate $\eta$.

\paragraph{Magnitude.}
% It remains to choose the step magnitude. 
% Using the LMO budgets $\tau_A$ and $\tau_B$ directly would tie the magnitude to the LMO geometry rather than to the merged-weight update.
We now choose the update magnitudes for the two factors subject to two constraints. First, the linearized merged update $\Delta W$ should have a spectral norm bounded by a user-specified learning rate $\eta$. Second, the factor updates $\Delta A$ and $\Delta B$ should have equal spectral-norm magnitude.

By the triangle inequality and submultiplicativity,
\begin{equation}
\norm{\Delta W}_2
\approx
\norm{B\Delta A+\Delta B A}_2
\le
\norm{B}_2\norm{\Delta A}_2
+
\norm{A}_2\norm{\Delta B}_2 .
\label{eq:ours-prodcap}
\end{equation}

Treating $A$ and $B$ symmetrically, we use a single step size $\rho$ for both factors, setting $\norm{\Delta A}_2=\norm{\Delta B}_2=\rho$. Choosing
\begin{equation}
\rho = \frac{\eta}{\norm{A}_2+\norm{B}_2}
\label{eq:ours-rho}
\end{equation}
ensures $\norm{\Delta W}_2\leq \eta$, and gives the updates
\[
\Delta A = -\rho\,\frac{D_A}{\norm{D_A}_2},
\qquad
\Delta B = -\rho\,\frac{D_B}{\norm{D_B}_2}.
\]
Including the dropped quadratic term $\Delta B\Delta A$, the actual merged update overshoots $\eta$ by at most a relative $\eta/(\norm{A}_2+\norm{B}_2)^2$ (\Cref{rem:quadratic}).
\Cref{app:gauge} examines how the step size $\rho$ depends on the relative scale of $A$ and $B$.
\paragraph{Optimizer step.}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:ours} summarizes the resulting step. The direction formula
\eqref{eq:factor-directions} acts on Nesterov momentum rather than raw factor
gradients.
The step uses Gram Newton--Schulz iterations for the matrix sign and inverse
square roots (\Cref{app:gramns}), power iteration for spectral norms
(\Cref{alg:smax}), and relative damping in the curvature inverse square roots
(\Cref{app:damping}). For tractability, the curvature metrics are diagonal, as
in Adafactor \cite{adafactor}. Their diagonal entries are stored as EMA vectors
$p$ and $q$, initialized to a small multiple of the all-ones vector so that the
first step, before any curvature is observed, uses the identity metric. Before
the direction step, the algorithm rescales each by its largest entry,
$\bar P=\diag(p/\norm{p}_\infty)$ and
$\bar Q=\diag(q/\norm{q}_\infty)$, and uses $\bar P,\bar Q$ in the direction.
For a single undamped step this normalization only rescales $D_A$ and $D_B$, so
it has no effect after the spectral normalization in \eqref{eq:ours-rho}. With
EMA, however, the normalization fixes the scale ambiguity of the Kronecker
factorization before each minibatch estimate for $p$ and $q$, so $\beta_2$ sets
the EMA decay rather than an arbitrary split of scale between the two vectors.
\Cref{app:matnormal} derives this normalized second-moment estimator.

\nikhil{$C_B$ should align with $\bar{Q}$, vertical spacing is uneven between the sections and within sections}
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\methodname{} step for one LoRA pair $(A,B)$}
\label{alg:ours}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Statex \textbf{Hyperparameters:} learning rate $\eta$, momentum decay $\beta_1$, curvature decay $\beta_2$
\Statex
\Phase{Nesterov Momentum}
\State $\begin{aligned}[t]
  M_A &\gets \beta_1 M_A+(1-\beta_1)G_A, &\quad M_B &\gets \beta_1 M_B+(1-\beta_1)G_B\\[2pt]
  \widehat M_A &\gets \beta_1 M_A+(1-\beta_1)G_A, &\quad \widehat M_B &\gets \beta_1 M_B+(1-\beta_1)G_B
\end{aligned}$ \Comment{buffer + look-ahead}
\Statex
\Phase{Direction}
\State $\bar P\gets\diag\!\big(p/\norm{p}_\infty\big), \qquad \bar Q\gets\diag\!\big(q/\norm{q}_\infty\big)$
\State $C_A\gets B^\top \bar P\, B, \qquad C_B\gets A\,\bar Q\, A^\top$
\State $D_A\gets C_A^{-1/2}\,\msign\big(C_A^{-1/2}\,\widehat M_A\,\bar Q^{-1/2}\big)\,\bar Q^{-1/2}$ \Comment{preconditioned polar step~\eqref{eq:factor-directions}}
\State $D_B\gets \bar P^{-1/2}\,\msign\big(\bar P^{-1/2}\,\widehat M_B\,C_B^{-1/2}\big)\,C_B^{-1/2}$
\Statex
\Phase{Magnitude}
\State $\rho\gets\eta/(\norm{A}_2+\norm{B}_2)$ \Comment{spectral-norm control~\eqref{eq:ours-rho}}
\State $A\gets A-\rho\,D_A/\norm{D_A}_2, \qquad B\gets B-\rho\,D_B/\norm{D_B}_2$
\Statex
\Phase{Metric Update}
\State $q\gets\beta_2\, q+(1-\beta_2)\diag\big(G_A^\top C_A^{-1} G_A\big)/r$ \Comment{coupled estimator~\eqref{eq:ours-klcoupling}}
\State $p\gets\beta_2\, p+(1-\beta_2)\diag\big(G_B\, C_B^{-1} G_B^\top\big)/r$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

Here we evaluate \methodname{}, comparing it to several baselines in~\Cref{sec:exp-main}, investigating which design choices of \methodname{} are responsible for its efficacy in~\Cref{sec:exp-ablation}, and testing robustness across rank and an out-of-distribution finetuning task in~\Cref{sec:exp-taskdep}.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

\paragraph{Comparison.}
For every model, dataset, and rank, we only vary the optimizer and learning rate. All methods use LoRA on every linear layer except the output layer, with the standard initialization $B=0$ and random $A$ \cite{lora}. We set the LoRA scaling $\alpha=r$, following best practice \cite{loralearnsless, raschka}, so the $\alpha/r$ factor in \eqref{eq:lora} is one. For each optimizer, we sweep the learning rate under a constant schedule with no weight decay and select the run with the lowest held-out evaluation loss.

The main comparisons finetune OLMo-2-1B~\cite{olmo2}, Llama-3.2-1B~\cite{llama3}, Qwen2.5-1.5B~\cite{qwen25}, and Llama-3-8B on code (OpenCoder \cite{opencoder}) and math (OpenMathInstruct-2 \cite{openmathinstruct2}) datasets, using rank $r=256$ unless stated otherwise. Additional experiments in \Cref{sec:exp-taskdep} vary the rank as well as change the finetuning dataset to a low-resource language. \Cref{app:exp} gives the full experimental configurations.

\paragraph{Baselines.} We compare \methodname{} (\Cref{sec:method}) to the following optimizers:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Adam} \cite{adam} --- our baseline, applied independently to the two factors $A$ and $B$.
  \item \textbf{Muon} \cite{muon} --- the Muon update~\eqref{eq:lmo} applied independently to each factor.
  \item \textbf{iMuon} \cite{imuon} --- the spectral LMO~\eqref{eq:imuon-lmo-ambient} applied to momentum in the product space.
  \item \textbf{LoRA-RITE} \cite{lorarite} --- an adaptive, transformation-invariant LoRA optimizer that preconditions the factor gradients in a rotation-invariant basis.
\end{itemize}
We use the authors' official implementations for iMuon and LoRA-RITE, and detail every optimizer's settings in \Cref{app:exp}.


\subsection{Measuring Efficiency Benefits}
\label{sec:exp-main}
We score each optimizer by its \emph{steps-to-Adam}: the number of steps a tuned run needs to match Adam's tuned loss after $T$ steps. Across our experiments, \methodname{} reaches Adam's loss in $1.2$--$1.7\times$ fewer steps (\Cref{tab:breadth}). On Llama-3.2-1B finetuned on math, \methodname{} also outperforms Muon, iMuon, and LoRA-RITE, none of which improves over Adam (\Cref{fig:hero}). Additional learning curves omitted in the main text can be found in \Cref{app:curves}.



\paragraph{Wall-clock.}\label{sec:exp-walltime} The total FLOPs of each step of \methodname{}   are independent of the batch size, while the forward
and backward passes scale with batch size (\Cref{app:flops}). Thus the  relative per-step time of \methodname{}  shrinks
as the batch grows. At our batch size (\Cref{app:exp}) it adds at most $3\%$ over Adam on
all four base models (\Cref{tab:breadth}), and the step-count speedup carries to wall-clock almost
unchanged.

\subsection{Component Ablation}
\label{sec:exp-ablation}

\methodname{} can be decomposed into three components.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.7em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Product-awareness}. The product-aware LMO in~\eqref{eq:decoupled-lora-lmo}, which acts on the merged product $BA$ rather than on the factors $A$ and $B$ separately.
  \item \textbf{Curvature}. The adaptively estimated diagonal curvature weights $\bar P$ and $\bar Q$ in~\eqref{eq:ours-klcoupling}.
  \item \textbf{Magnitude}. Sets each factor update to spectral norm $\rho$ in~\eqref{eq:ours-rho}.
\end{itemize}
With the diagonal metrics set to identity ($P = I_{\mathrm{out}}$ and $Q=I_{\mathrm{in}}$) and magnitude control removed, \methodname{} reduces to Product Muon. This update coincides with the momentum-free forms of LoRA-Muon, iMuon, and LoRA-RITE, as shown in~\Cref{app:memoryless}.

On Llama-3.2-1B finetuned on math, we ablate the curvature metric and the magnitude control in turn to measure each component's contribution to \methodname{}'s speedup over Adam (\Cref{fig:ablation}).
Removing both components eliminates the speedup over Adam in the right panel of~\Cref{fig:ablation}. Restoring the curvature metric and then the magnitude control each recovers about half of \methodname{}'s speedup.

\section{Prior Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Hyperparameter rescaling for LoRA.}
LoRA~\cite{lora} enables parameter-efficient finetuning by freezing the pretrained
weights and training only a low-rank update~\eqref{eq:lora} through factors $A$ and
$B$, typically optimized with Adam~\cite{adam}. Several recent methods refine this
approach by rescaling hyperparameters such as the learning rates, adapter scale, or
initialization variance to account for the low-rank structure. Motivated by large-width
feature-learning arguments, LoRA+~\cite{loraplus} assigns $B$ a larger learning rate
than $A$, and follow-up work~\cite{hayou2024initialization, li2025beyond} extends this
to other initializations. Rank-stabilized LoRA~\cite{rslora} replaces the $\alpha/r$
adapter scale with $\alpha/\sqrt{r}$ to avoid the rank-dependent shrinkage that makes
high-rank adapters hard to train, and $\mu$A~\cite{mua} studies how these
hyperparameters should scale with rank to keep the optimal learning rate stable. In
contrast, \methodname{} ensures each factor receives an update of equal size in the spectral norm, while simultaneously ensuring the merged-weight update is also bounded in spectral norm.

\paragraph{Matrix-aware updates.}
Recently, optimizers that operate at the matrix level, rather than treating parameters as
a flattened vector, have attracted attention for their gains over Adam~\cite{liu2025muon,
qiu2026hyperparameter, benchmark_practices}. Muon~\cite{muon} solves the spectral-norm LMO
for a dense weight, producing an update equal to the matrix sign of the momentum
gradient~\cite{polarexpress} (\Cref{lem:muon-lmo}). Shampoo~\cite{shampoo},
SOAP~\cite{soap}, and KL-Shampoo~\cite{klshampoo} estimate the gradient curvature as
Kronecker-factored second moments. Mousse~\cite{mousse} uses such a second moment estimate to
whiten the gradient before a matrix-sign step (\Cref{app:related-spectral}). \methodname{} also applies a matrix-sign step to a preconditioned gradient. Its metric is derived from controlling per-sample loss change in the batch. We adapt this update to LoRA training and add magnitude control, which removes the need for learning-rate grafting \cite{shi2023distributed, mousse}.

\paragraph{Product-invariant LoRA optimizers.}
The learned update $BA$ is a product of two matrices, so it is determined only up to the
change of basis $(A,B)\mapsto(NA,BN^{-1})$ for invertible $N$. Since Adam is not invariant
to this transformation, a line of work has proposed LoRA optimizers that are. LoRA-RITE
removes the basis dependence with an adaptive preconditioner~\cite{lorarite}, while Riemannion
optimizes $BA$ directly on the fixed-rank matrix manifold~\cite{riemannion}. iMuon and
LoRA-Muon derive invariant updates from spectral-norm
LMOs~\cite{imuon,loramuon}. As discussed in \Cref{app:memoryless}, the momentum-free LoRA-RITE, LoRA-Muon,
and iMuon LMO updates all coincide with \eqref{eq:decoupled-lora-lmo}. \methodname{} does not satisfy this invariance, but it outperforms iMuon in our experiments (\Cref{sec:exp-main}), suggesting that invariance is not the limiting factor in this setting.

\paragraph{Magnitude control for low-rank products.}
Product Muon~\eqref{eq:decoupled-lora-lmo} bounds $\norm{B\Delta A}_2$ and $\norm{\Delta B A}_2$ but not the factor steps $\norm{\Delta A}_2$ and $\norm{\Delta B}_2$. As a result, when $A$ or $B$ have small singular values, the updates $\Delta A$ and $\Delta B$ can be very large, which can destabilize training. Spectron~\cite{spectron} and QuacK~\cite{quack}, which are not based on the LMO step~\eqref{eq:decoupled-lora-lmo},
explicitly control the factor steps by scaling them with the factor norms. Spectron rescales both steps to spectral norm $\eta/(\norm{A}_2+\norm{B}_2+1)$, and QuacK scales each factor's learning rate by the inverse norm of the other. Similar to Spectron, \methodname{} rescales both factor steps to a common spectral norm $\rho=\eta/(\norm{A}_2+\norm{B}_2)$ (\Cref{rem:quadratic}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\methodname{} takes a different route to optimizing LoRA adapters: it asks how  a step should look through the merged product weight and through the current batch loss, rather than treating the two LoRA factors as unrelated parameter matrices. This view leads to a practical optimizer that combines a product-aware spectral direction, a lightweight curvature metric, and explicit control of factor-step magnitudes. In our experiments, this was enough to turn matrix-aware LoRA optimization into a consistent training-speed improvement over Adam, with small overhead and good learning-rate transfer across rank.

The method is still built on approximations. We use diagonal curvature factors for efficiency, so \methodname{} may miss interactions captured by richer Kronecker or full-matrix metrics. The derivation also relies on a linearized loss change and a linearized product update, which means the optimizer does not directly control higher-order effects or the dropped $\Delta B\Delta A$ term. Finally, our evaluation focuses on LoRA finetuning for language models and on held-out loss as the main measure of progress; broader downstream evaluations, longer training horizons, other adapter families, and different hardware or batch-size regimes remain open.

\rob{**HERE**}
These limitations point to several future directions. One is to design better outergradient approximations that remain cheap enough for adapter training. Another is to incorporate second-order or trust-region information without losing the simplicity of the current update. More broadly, the per-sample loss-control perspective may be useful beyond LoRA, especially for other parameter-efficient methods where the optimizer acts on a factorized or constrained parameterization but the model only sees the composed update.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

This appendix proves the results used in \Cref{sec:method}, in their order of appearance there. \Cref{lem:muon-lmo} is the spectral-norm LMO whose solution gives Muon's update direction (\Cref{sec:muon}). \Cref{lem:gradprop} collects the gradient properties, and \Cref{lem:pseudogradient-spectral} evaluates the resulting outergradient support function under a Kronecker-factored metric; together they turn the per-sample loss constraint of \Cref{sec:sample-loss-lmo} into a preconditioned spectral norm. \Cref{lem:whitened-spectral-lmo} then solves the preconditioned spectral-norm LMO that sets the \methodname{} direction. Finally, \Cref{rem:quadratic} bounds the error from linearizing the merged update.

\begin{lemma}[Spectral norm LMO]\label{lem:muon-lmo}
Let $G\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and $\eta>0$, and write
$\norm{G}_{\mathrm{nuc}}=\sum_i\sigma_i(G)$ for the nuclear norm, the sum of the
singular values. Then
\[
    \min_{\norm{X}_2\leq \eta}\ip{G,X}
    =
    -\eta\norm{G}_{\mathrm{nuc}},
\]
and $X^\star = -\eta\msign(G)$ is the unique minimizer of least Frobenius norm.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{lem:muon-lmo}]
We lower-bound the objective by norm duality and exhibit a feasible point that
attains it. By duality of the spectral and nuclear norms, every feasible $X$
satisfies
\[
    \ip{G,X}
    \geq
    -\norm{G}_{\mathrm{nuc}}\norm{X}_2
    \geq
    -\eta\norm{G}_{\mathrm{nuc}}.
\]
Let $G=USV^\top$ be a reduced singular value decomposition, so
$S=\diag(\sigma_i(G))$. The choice
\[
    X=-\eta UV^\top=-\eta\msign(G)
\]
is feasible and satisfies
\[
    \ip{G,X}
    =
    -\eta\Tr(S)
    =
    -\eta\norm{G}_{\mathrm{nuc}},
\]
so it attains the lower bound and is therefore optimal. The minimum-norm
statement follows from Lemma C.2~\cite{parshakova2026muon}.
\end{proof}



\gradprop*
\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{lem:gradprop}]
For the {\bf Leverage bound}, let $g_i=\vec(G_i)$ and
$M=[g_1,\ldots,g_n]$, so $g_i = Me_i$ for every $i =1,\ldots, n$. Then
\[
\ip{\Sigma^\dagger g_i, g_i} = ng_i^\top (MM^\top)^{\dagger} g_i = n e_i^\top M^\top  (MM^\top)^{\dagger} M e_i  \leq n,
\]
since $M^\top  (MM^\top)^{\dagger} M$ is the orthogonal projector onto the range
of $M^\top$.

The {\bf Rank one} property is the chain rule: $W$ encodes a linear layer
$x_i \mapsto W x_i$, so
\[
\nabla \ell_{x_i}(Wx_i) =  \left. \frac{d }{dz} \ell_{x_i}(z)
\right|_{z =Wx_i} x_i^\top
=b_i x_i^\top,
\]
where $b_i = \left.\frac{d }{dz} \ell_{x_i}(z)  \right|_{z =Wx_i}$ is the
output-side gradient.
\end{proof}



\begin{lemma}[Outergradient set under a Kronecker factorization]
\label{lem:pseudogradient-spectral}
Let
$P\in\R^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{out}}}$ and
$Q\in\R^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ be symmetric positive definite,
and let $\Sigma=Q\otimes P$. Recall the outergradient set~\eqref{eq:outgrad},
\[
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)=\bigl\{\, bx^\top ~\big|~ b\in\R^{d_{\mathrm{out}}},\ x\in\R^{d_{\mathrm{in}}},\ \ip{\Sigma^\dagger\vec(bx^\top),\vec(bx^\top)}\leq n \,\bigr\}.
\]
Then for every $\Delta W\in\R^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$,
\[
  \max_{\tilde G \in \mathcal{G}(\Sigma)} \ip{ \tilde G , \Delta W}
  =\sqrt{n}\norm{P^{1/2}\Delta W Q^{1/2}}_2 .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{lem:pseudogradient-spectral}]
We evaluate the support function by a change of variables that turns the
constraint into a product of Euclidean balls. Since $P$ and $Q$ are positive
definite, $\Sigma^\dagger=\Sigma^{-1}=Q^{-1}\otimes P^{-1}$. For
$bx^\top\in\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)$, column-major vectorization gives
$\vec(bx^\top)=x\otimes b$, so
\[
\ip{\Sigma^\dagger\vec(bx^\top),\vec(bx^\top)}
=(x\otimes b)^\top(Q^{-1}\otimes P^{-1})(x\otimes b)
=(x^\top Q^{-1}x)(b^\top P^{-1}b).
\]
Writing $b=P^{1/2}u$ and $x=Q^{1/2}v$, the constraint becomes
$\norm{u}_2^2\norm{v}_2^2\leq n$ and the objective becomes
\[
\ip{bx^\top,\Delta W}=b^\top\Delta W x=u^\top P^{1/2}\Delta W Q^{1/2}v .
\]
Maximizing over the balls gives the spectral norm,
\[
\max_{\tilde G\in\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)}\ip{\tilde G,\Delta W}
=\max_{\norm{u}_2\norm{v}_2\leq\sqrt{n}}u^\top P^{1/2}\Delta W Q^{1/2}v
=\sqrt{n}\,\norm{P^{1/2}\Delta W Q^{1/2}}_2. \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\GeneralLmoUpdate*
\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{lem:whitened-spectral-lmo}]
We change variables to reduce the preconditioned LMO to the plain spectral-norm
LMO of \Cref{lem:muon-lmo}. Set $Y=LXR$, so $X=L^{-1}YR^{-1}$; the constraint
becomes $\norm{Y}_2\le\tau$ and the objective becomes
\[
\ip{M,X}=\ip{L^{-1}MR^{-1},Y} .
\]
By \Cref{lem:muon-lmo} with budget $\tau$, the unique least Frobenius-norm
minimizer is
\[
    Y=-\tau\,\msign(L^{-1}MR^{-1}).
\]
Substituting $X=L^{-1}YR^{-1}$ gives the stated solution.
\end{proof}



\begin{remark}[Second-order term in the merged update]
\label{rem:quadratic}
The magnitude rule bounds the \emph{linearized} merged update; here we check that
restoring the second-order term dropped in that linearization leaves the bound
essentially unchanged. Writing $s=\norm{A}_2+\norm{B}_2$, the
rule~\eqref{eq:ours-rho} sets $\rho=\eta/s$ to bound the linearized update
$B\Delta A+\Delta B A$ of~\eqref{eq:DeltaW-lin}. The actual update also contains
the quadratic term $\Delta B\Delta A$ dropped there,
\[
  \Delta W = B\Delta A+\Delta B A+\Delta B\Delta A .
\]
With $\norm{\Delta A}_2=\norm{\Delta B}_2=\rho$, the triangle inequality and
submultiplicativity give
\[
  \norm{\Delta W}_2\le\rho s+\rho^2=\eta\Bigl(1+\tfrac{\eta}{s^2}\Bigr),
\]
so the actual update exceeds the target $\eta$ by at most the relative amount
$\eta/s^2$. At the standard initialization $B=0$ with $A$ having independent
entries of variance $\Theta(1/d_{\mathrm{in}})$, we have $s=\norm{A}_2=\Theta(1)$,
so the overshoot is $O(\eta)$ and shrinks as the factors grow.
\end{remark}



\section{Momentum-Free LoRA-RITE, iMuon, and LoRA-Muon}
\label{app:memoryless}
LoRA-RITE \cite{lorarite}, iMuon \cite{imuon}, and LoRA-Muon \cite{loramuon}
reduce to Product Muon~\eqref{eq:decoupled-lora-lmo} once their moment accumulation is removed. We show this below.


\paragraph{Setup.} As in \Cref{sec:method}, 
let $G=\nabla_W \mathcal{L}(W) \big |_{W=BA}$ be the product
gradient, $G_A=B^\top G$ and $G_B=GA^\top$ be the factor gradients, 
and $S_B=AA^\top$ and
$S_A=B^\top B$ be the $r\times r$ Gram matrices.
The two factorwise LMOs in Product Muon~\eqref{eq:decoupled-lora-lmo} are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \Delta A &\in    \argmin_{\Delta A}~ \ip{G_A, \Delta A} \quad \textnormal{subject to}\quad
\norm{B \Delta A}_2\le\tau \\
\Delta B &\in   \argmin_{\Delta B}~ \ip{G_B,  \Delta B} \quad \textnormal{subject to}\quad  \norm{\Delta B A}_2\le\tau.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:decoupled-lora-lmo-app} 
\end{equation}
They have the closed-form solution
\begin{align} 
    \Delta A &=-\tau\,S_A^{-1/2}\msign(S_A^{-1/2}G_A) \nonumber \\
    \Delta B &=-\tau\,\msign(G_BS_B^{-1/2})S_B^{-1/2}.  \label{eq:decoupled-lora-lmo-closed}
\end{align}
We will use the fact that for any matrix $M$ with full column rank and reduced SVD $M=U\Sigma V^\top$,
the matrix sign equals
\begin{equation}
  % Not true unless we assume full rank
  M(M^\top M)^{-1/2}=
  UV^\top=\msign(M).
  \label{eq:polar-id}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{LoRA-RITE.}
Algorithm~1 of LoRA-RITE~\cite{lorarite} adds three pieces of memory to a base step. These are an EMA first moment with parameter $\beta_1$, an accumulated second moment transported across the changing factor basis, and an ``escaped-mass'' floor added to that second moment.
Dropping all three, we get the authors' momentum-free update (their Eq.~(14)),
which in our notation for $ \widehat G_B=G_B S_B^{-1/2}$ becomes
\[
  \Delta B=-\eta \widehat G_B (\widehat G_B^\top\widehat G_B)^{-1/2} S_B^{-1/2}.
\]
With no accumulation by \eqref{eq:polar-id} the middle factor is the matrix sign, so
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta B=-\eta \msign(\widehat G_B) S_B^{-1/2}
  =-\eta \msign \big(G_B S_B^{-1/2}\big) S_B^{-1/2},
\end{equation*}
which is equal to the $B$-update given in~\eqref{eq:decoupled-lora-lmo-closed}. Analogous arguments hold for the $A$-update.


\paragraph{iMuon, momentum-free.}
iMuon relies on a choice of \emph{ambient matrix} $H\in\R^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$, which is the dense-space signal passed to the intrinsic spectral LMO. The intrinsic spectral LMO of Corollary~4.1 maps this ambient matrix to the factor updates as follows
\begin{equation}
  \Delta B(H) =\msign \big(HA^\top S_B^{-1/2}\big)S_B^{-1/2},\qquad
  \Delta A(H) =S_A^{-1/2}\msign \big(S_A^{-1/2}B^\top H\big).
  \label{eq:imuon-lmo-ambient}
\end{equation}
For the base Algorithm~1 in~\cite{imuon}, the ambient matrix is the raw gradient $G$, so~\eqref{eq:imuon-lmo-ambient}
is again equivalent to \eqref{eq:decoupled-lora-lmo-closed}. Our experiments benchmark the official iMuon implementation from Appendix~K of~\cite{imuon}, which differs from this momentum-free decoupled update.

\paragraph{iMuon, with momentum.} The official implementation of iMuon with momentum (their Appendix~K) does not
use the gradient as input to \eqref{eq:imuon-lmo-ambient}. 
It instead first combines the two factor
look-aheads $\tilde G_A=G_A+\beta m_A$ and $\tilde G_B=G_B+\beta m_B$ (buffers $m_A,m_B$,
with parameter $\beta$) into a single ambient matrix, then uses that matrix as input
\begin{equation*}
  \widehat M=\tilde G_B A+B\tilde G_A .
\end{equation*}
Turning momentum off ($\beta=0$) removes the buffers but not the recombination, i.e.,
\begin{equation*}
  \widehat M=G_B A+B G_A=G\,A^\top A+BB^\top G\ \neq\ G .
\end{equation*}
Plugging $\widehat M$ into \eqref{eq:imuon-lmo-ambient} and using $G_A A^\top=B^\top G_B$,
\begin{equation*}
  \widehat M A^\top=G_B S_B+(BB^\top)G_B
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \Delta B(\widehat M)=\msign\!\big(G_B S_B^{1/2}+(BB^\top)G_B S_B^{-1/2}\big)S_B^{-1/2},
\end{equation*}
which is different from Product Muon. The argument for $\Delta A$ is symmetric.
Thus switching momentum off does not recover \eqref{eq:decoupled-lora-lmo}. While
\eqref{eq:decoupled-lora-lmo-app} is the
momentum-free limit of iMuon's theory (Corollary~4.1), 
it is not the momentum-free limit of its implemented optimizer.


\paragraph{LoRA-Muon.}
The concurrent LoRA-Muon paper~\cite{loramuon} also arrives at the two decoupled
factorwise LMOs in~\eqref{eq:decoupled-lora-lmo}.
Specifically, its momentum-free LMOs in Eq. (7) and (8) of~\cite{loramuon} are precisely \eqref{eq:decoupled-lora-lmo}.
LoRA-Muon also shows that this
Gram-matrix form is equivalent to the simplified QR-coordinate form of
LoRA-RITE~\cite{loramuon}.



\section{Extended Prior Work: Spectral and Curvature-Aware Optimizers}
\label{app:related-spectral}
Here we will better contextualize how
 \methodname{} relates to other spectral and curvature-aware
optimizers (\Cref{sec:related}).

\paragraph{Mousse and the whitening power.}
Mousse fixes its trust region by a Shampoo curvature $H\approx(R\otimes L)^{1/2}$, with
$L$ and $R$ being the moving averages of $GG^\top$ and $G^\top G$. Whitening by
$H^{1/2}=(R\otimes L)^{1/4}$ gives the dense update
\[
  \Delta W=-L^{-1/4}\,\msign\!\big(L^{-1/4} G R^{-1/4}\big)\,R^{-1/4}.
\]
\methodname{} instead uses the full gradient covariance $\Sigma\approx Q\otimes P$ as its
metric, preconditioning by $P^{-1/2}$ and $Q^{-1/2}$ (\Cref{alg:ours}), an inverse-square-root
rather than Mousse's fourth-root power; the first-order LoRA product map then contracts
these dense factors to $C_A=B^\top P B$ and $C_B=AQA^\top$.

\paragraph{SOAP-Muon.}
SOAP-Muon whitens the gradient with a SOAP preconditioner and then orthogonalizes the
result \cite{soapmuon}.

\paragraph{Kang et al.}
For spectral gradient flow in a simplified matrix-factorization model,
Kang et al.~\cite{kang_specgf} prove that the singular values of the merged adapter
product grow at a nearly uniform rate even though the two factors are orthogonalized
independently.

\paragraph{Spectron.}
Spectron \cite{spectron} bounds the induced product update by rescaling orthogonalized
factor steps with the current factor norms, in native low-rank pretraining. It is close in
spirit to \methodname{}'s magnitude control but is not curvature-aware and is not derived
from a single LoRA LMO.

\section{Estimating Normalized Second Moments}
\label{app:matnormal}
For one LoRA layer, the curvature quantity we would like to use is the second
moment of the gradient with respect to the merged weight $W=W_0+BA$.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:ours} cannot store that full matrix, so it keeps two
diagonal vectors, one for output coordinates and one for input coordinates. This
section defines the recency-weighted second moment, writes the normalized
Kronecker fixed point for these two vectors, and shows how the online update
takes one relaxed step toward that fixed point.

\paragraph{Weighted historical covariance.}
At step $s$, let $x_{s,i}$ be an input activation to the layer. The merged
weight, per-example gradient, minibatch second moment, and raw EMA are
\[
\begin{aligned}
  W_s &= W_0+B_sA_s,\\
  G_{s,i} &= \nabla_W\ell_{x_{s,i}}(W_sx_{s,i}),
  \qquad
  g_{s,i} = \vec(G_{s,i}),\\
  \Sigma_s &= \frac1{n_s}\sum_i g_{s,i}g_{s,i}^\top,\\
  V_{s+1} &= \beta_2V_s+(1-\beta_2)\Sigma_s,
  \qquad
  V_0=0 .
\end{aligned}
\]
Here $n_s$ is the minibatch size and $\beta_2$ is the curvature decay
coefficient.

The EMA is equivalent to a covariance over a recency-weighted finite set of past
examples. For $s<t$, define
\[
\begin{aligned}
  w_{t,s,i}
  &=
  \frac{(1-\beta_2)\beta_2^{t-1-s}}{(1-\beta_2^t)n_s},\\
  \mathbb{E}_t[\phi(G)]
  &=
  \sum_{s<t}\sum_{i=1}^{n_s}
  w_{t,s,i}\phi(G_{s,i}),\\
  \bar\Sigma_t
  &=
  \mathbb{E}_t[gg^\top]
  =
  \sum_{s<t}\sum_{i=1}^{n_s}
  w_{t,s,i} g_{s,i}g_{s,i}^\top .
\end{aligned}
\]
The weights sum to one and $V_t=(1-\beta_2^t)\bar\Sigma_t$. The scalar
$1-\beta_2^t$ only changes the overall scale, so it does not affect the
normalized factors below. The same covariance view gives the weighted version
of the leverage argument from the main text:
with $z_{t,s,i}=\sqrt{w_{t,s,i}}\,g_{s,i}$, the covariance is
$\bar\Sigma_t=\sum z_{t,s,i}z_{t,s,i}^\top$, so the same leverage bound applies
to the rescaled gradients $z_{t,s,i}$, or equivalently to
$\sqrt{w_{t,s,i}}\ip{G_{s,i},\Delta W}$.

\paragraph{Kronecker model.}
The Kronecker approximation is applied to this weighted covariance:
\begin{equation}\label{e-diag-kron-second-mom-model}
  \bar\Sigma_t
  \approx
  \kappa_t(\bar Q_t\otimes\bar P_t),
  \qquad
  \bar P_t=\diag(\bar p_t),\quad
  \bar Q_t=\diag(\bar q_t),\quad
  \norm{\bar p_t}_\infty=\norm{\bar q_t}_\infty=1 .
\end{equation}
The normalization is a convention. Replacing $\bar P_t$ by $a\bar P_t$ and
$\bar Q_t$ by $a^{-1}\bar Q_t$ leaves $\bar Q_t\otimes\bar P_t$ unchanged. The
constraints above choose one representative and leave $\kappa_t$ as the
remaining scalar.

\paragraph{Fixed-point map.}
At the current factors $A=A_t$ and $B=B_t$, a merged-weight gradient $G$ induces
factor gradients $G_A=B^\top G$ and $G_B=G A^\top$. For normalized vectors
$\bar p$ and $\bar q$, define
\[
\begin{aligned}
  T_Q(\bar p)
  &=
  \frac1r\diag\!\left(
    \mathbb{E}_t[G_A^\top (B^\top\diag(\bar p)B)^{-1}G_A]
  \right),\\
  T_P(\bar q)
  &=
  \frac1r\diag\!\left(
    \mathbb{E}_t[G_B (A\diag(\bar q)A^\top)^{-1}G_B^\top]
  \right).
\end{aligned}
\]
Here $\mathbb{E}_t$ denotes the weighted average with weights $w_{t,s,i}$ from
the previous paragraph. If the Kronecker model holds and
$\bar p=\bar p_t$, then the first map gives $\kappa_t\bar q_t$: the expectation
contains $B^\top\bar P_tB$, and the inverse contributes
$\Tr((B^\top\bar P_tB)^{-1}B^\top\bar P_tB)=r$. The argument for $T_P$ is the
same with $A$ and $B$ swapped. The normalized KL-Shampoo fixed point is
\[
  q=T_Q\!\left(\frac{p}{\norm{p}_\infty}\right),
  \qquad
  p=T_P\!\left(\frac{q}{\norm{q}_\infty}\right),
  \qquad
  \bar q=\frac{q}{\norm{q}_\infty},\quad
  \bar p=\frac{p}{\norm{p}_\infty}.
\]

\paragraph{Online estimator.}
\methodname{} performs one relaxed stochastic step toward this fixed point per
optimizer step. It first normalizes the stored vectors,
$\bar P_t=\diag(p_t/\norm{p_t}_\infty)$ and
$\bar Q_t=\diag(q_t/\norm{q_t}_\infty)$, then forms
$C_A=B^\top\bar P_tB$ and $C_B=A\bar Q_tA^\top$. On the current minibatch, the
estimates and EMA updates are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ours-klcoupling}
\begin{aligned}
  \widehat q_t
  &= \frac1r\diag\!\left(G_A^\top C_A^{-1}G_A\right),
  &
  q_{t+1}
  &= \beta_2q_t+(1-\beta_2)\widehat q_t,\\
  \widehat p_t
  &= \frac1r\diag\!\left(G_B C_B^{-1}G_B^\top\right),
  &
  p_{t+1}
  &= \beta_2p_t+(1-\beta_2)\widehat p_t .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The key point is that the EMA is taken before normalization. The vector $q_t$
estimates $\kappa_t\bar q_t$, and $p_t$ estimates $\kappa_t\bar p_t$. Normalizing each
minibatch estimate first would average per-minibatch normalized factors, which
is not the normalized factor of the averaged historical covariance. The next
direction step therefore uses $q_{t+1}/\norm{q_{t+1}}_\infty$ and
$p_{t+1}/\norm{p_{t+1}}_\infty$.

\paragraph{Why normalization comes first.}
The normalization must happen before constructing $C_A$ and $C_B$. If
$P=a\bar P$ replaces $\bar P$ in $B^\top\bar P B$, then
$(B^\top P B)^{-1}=a^{-1}(B^\top\bar P B)^{-1}$, so the resulting $q$ estimate
is divided by $a$. Across time, the contribution from step $s$ would be weighted
by $(1-\beta_2)\beta_2^{t-1-s}/a_s$ instead of
$(1-\beta_2)\beta_2^{t-1-s}$. Normalizing first makes $\beta_2$ set the EMA
decay, rather than the arbitrary Kronecker representative.

\section{Numerical Subroutines}
\label{app:primitives}

\subsection{Spectral-Norm Estimation}
\label{app:smax}
\Cref{alg:ours} requires repeated estimates of spectral norms.  
We estimate each $\norm{M}_2$ by warm-started power iteration on the smaller of
the two Gram matrices (\Cref{alg:smax}), caching the leading vector across
optimizer steps so it starts near the top singular vector.  A cold or stale start
can under-estimate $\norm{M}_2$; since the optimizer divides by this estimate,
that would inflate the update and risk destabilizing training.  We therefore
return the larger of the power-iteration value and the largest row norm of $M$, a
lower bound on $\norm{M}_2$.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Guarded spectral-norm estimate of $\norm{M}_2$}
\label{alg:smax}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, $m\le n$ (else $M^\top$); cached $v\in\mathbb{R}^m$; iterations $K$
\State $l \gets \max_i\norm{M_{i,:}}_2$
\If{$v$ is empty} \State $v\gets M\mathbf{1}$ \Comment{cold start} \EndIf
\For{$k=1,\dots,K$} \Comment{iterate the smaller Gram $MM^\top$}
  \State $w\gets M^\top v$;\quad $v\gets Mw/\norm{Mw}_2$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\max\big\{\norm{M^\top v}_2,\ l\big\}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Matrix Sign and Inverse Square Root via Gram Newton--Schulz}
\label{app:gramns}

Beyond the spectral norm, \Cref{alg:ours} needs the matrix sign $\msign(\cdot)$
and the inverse square root of the $r \times r$ curvature matrix.  Both reduce to
an inverse square root of a small positive semidefinite matrix: the sign is
$\msign(X)=(XX^\top)^{-1/2}X$, and the curvature term is $C^{-1/2}$ directly.  We
compute that inverse square root by the Gram Newton--Schulz iteration
\cite{gram_ns} (\Cref{alg:gramns}), which uses only $r\times r$ matrix
multiplications and needs no eigendecomposition or SVD.

\paragraph{Quintic Newton--Schulz.}
Each iteration applies an odd degree-five polynomial
\begin{equation}\label{e-gram-ns-poly}
    p_t(s)
    =
    a_t s + b_t s^3 + c_t s^5 .
\end{equation}
The coefficients $(a_t,b_t,c_t)$ are the PolarExpress coefficients
\cite{polarexpress}.  They are chosen so that repeated composition of the
maps $p_t$ drives singular values toward $1$.  The coefficients are
computed once at startup and reused for every optimizer step.


\paragraph{Gram iteration.}
Rather than iterating on the rectangular matrix, we iterate on its $r\times r$
Gram matrix \cite{gram_ns}.  Let $R_t = Y_t Y_t^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times r}$
be the Gram matrix of the current rectangular iterate $Y_t$.  The rectangular
update and its Gram matrix evolve together in closed form,
\[
\begin{aligned}
    M_t &= a_t I + b_t R_t + c_t R_t^2, \\
    Y_{t+1} &= M_t Y_t, \\
    R_{t+1} &= Y_{t+1}Y_{t+1}^\top = M_t R_t M_t ,
\end{aligned}
\]
so the whole iteration runs on $R_t$ alone.  Because $M_t$ is a polynomial in
$R_t$, it shares the singular vectors of $Y_t$ and moves only its singular
values; since the coefficients \eqref{e-gram-ns-poly} drive the normalized
singular values to $1$, we have $Y_t \to \msign(Y_0)$.  The Gram update thus
implements the same quintic Newton--Schulz map on the singular values, at the
cost of only $r\times r$ matrix multiplications.

\paragraph{Shared accumulator.}
The method also maintains an accumulator $Q_t$.  Starting from
$Q_0=I$, each step sets
\[
    Q_{t+1}=M_t Q_t,
    \qquad
    R_{t+1}=M_t R_t M_t .
\]
Hence by induction,
\[
    Y_t=Q_tY_0,
    \qquad
    R_t=Q_tR_0Q_t^\top .
\]
Since the normalized singular values converge to $1$, $R_K\to I$, so
$Q_K\approx R_0^{-1/2}$.  The returned accumulator $Q=Q_K$ thus satisfies
$Q\approx(S/\gamma)^{-1/2}=\sqrt{\gamma}\,S^{-1/2}$, i.e.\ $S^{-1/2}\approx Q/\sqrt{\gamma}$.
The two uses differ only in the matrix $S$ and scalar $\gamma$ we pass to
\Cref{alg:gramns}:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Matrix sign.}
    Call \Cref{alg:gramns} with $S=XX^\top$ and $\gamma=\norm{X}_F^2$, giving
    \[
        \msign(X)=(XX^\top)^{-1/2}X=QX/\sqrt{\gamma}.
    \]
    \item \textbf{Inverse square root.}
    Damp $C\succ 0$ to $\hat C=C+\delta\lambda_{\max}(C)I$ (\Cref{app:damping}) and
    call \Cref{alg:gramns} with $S=\hat C$ and $\gamma=\Tr(\hat C)$, giving
    \[
        C^{-1/2}\approx\hat C^{-1/2}=Q/\sqrt{\gamma}.
    \]
\end{itemize}



\paragraph{Stability and precision.}
We run the Gram Newton--Schulz iteration in fp32, which is inexpensive because
every matrix is only $r\times r$.  We use $K=8$ iterations.


\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Gram Newton--Schulz iteration}
\label{alg:gramns}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require positive semidefinite $S \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times r}$; $\gamma \ge \lambda_{\max}(S)$; coefficients $\{(a_t,b_t,c_t)\}_{t=1}^{K}$
\Statex\vspace{-0.5\baselineskip}
\State $R\gets S/\gamma$;\quad $Q\gets I$ \Comment{normalize so $\lambda_{\max}(R)\le 1$}
\For{$t=1,\dots,K$}
  \State $M\gets a_t I+b_t R+c_t R^2$
  \State $Q\gets M Q$;\quad $R\gets M R M$
\EndFor
\State \Return $Q\approx (S/\gamma)^{-1/2}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{Relative Damping}\label{app:damping}
Each inverse square root in \Cref{alg:ours} is damped so that directions of
nearly zero curvature are not amplified without bound.  For a positive
semidefinite curvature matrix $C = U\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)U^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times r}$,
we damp by $\delta$ relative to the top eigenvalue; \Cref{alg:gramns} then
normalizes by $\gamma=\Tr(\hat C)$,
\[
    \hat C = C + \lambda_{\max}\delta I,
    \qquad
    \widetilde C = \hat C/\gamma .
\]
Since $\hat C \succ 0$, the trace $\gamma$, the normalized spectrum, and the resulting
inverse square root are all bounded,
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \lambda_{\max}(1+\delta) \ \le\ \gamma
        &= \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^r \lambda_i + r\lambda_{\max}\delta
        \ \le\ r\lambda_{\max}(1+\delta), \\[2pt]
    \frac{\lambda_i + \lambda_{\max}\delta}{\gamma}
        &\in \left[\frac{\delta}{r(1+\delta)},\ 1\right],
        \qquad
    \lambda\!\left(\widetilde C^{-1/2}\right)
        \in \left[1,\ \sqrt{\tfrac{r(1+\delta)}{\delta}}\right].
\end{aligned}
\]
The bound $\sqrt{r(1+\delta)/\delta}$ grows as $\delta\to 0$, so a smaller
$\delta$ permits stronger amplification of low-curvature directions.

\textbf{Warmup.}
At the beginning of training, an EMA curvature factor may still be zero.
When $\lambda_{\max}=0$, we set the inverse square root
to identity,
$\widetilde C^{-1/2}=I$.  The corresponding
side is then unpreconditioned, so the update reduces to the plain
momentum direction until the curvature estimate becomes nonzero.





\section{FLOP Overhead of the Optimizer}
\label{app:flops}
The wall-clock measurements in \Cref{sec:exp-walltime} depend on the hardware, so
we also report leading matmul FLOP counts, which do not.  We count one
multiply-add as two FLOPs.  The count below is for one
adapted linear layer with frozen base weight $W$ and rank-$r$ LoRA factors
$A,B$,
\[
  W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}},\quad
  B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r},\quad
  A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}},
\]
with $r\ll d_{\mathrm{in}},d_{\mathrm{out}}$.  Throughout, $K$ denotes the number
of Newton--Schulz iterations, and $n_{\mathrm{tok}}$ denotes the number of tokens
processed per optimization step.


\paragraph{Forward and backward.}
For a dense
layer $Y=XW^\top$, the forward pass costs
$2d_{\mathrm{in}}d_{\mathrm{out}}n_{\mathrm{tok}}$
FLOPs.
In full finetuning, given backward gradient $G=\nabla_Y \mathcal{L}$,
the backward pass computes both the input gradient and
the weight gradient, 
\[
\nabla_X \mathcal{L} = GW,
\qquad
\nabla_W \mathcal{L} = G^\top X,
\]
so it costs twice the forward.  In LoRA finetuning the
base weight $W$ is frozen, so we do not form
$\nabla_W \mathcal{L}$.  The
backward pass through $W$ therefore costs only one forward-equivalent
matmul:
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mathrm{fwd}}^{\mathrm{base}}
  =
  C_{\mathrm{bwd}}^{\mathrm{base}}
  =
  2 d_{\mathrm{in}}d_{\mathrm{out}} n_{\mathrm{tok}} .
  \label{eq:flop-fb}
\end{equation}
Thus the frozen base weights contribute
\[
    4N n_{\mathrm{tok}}
\]
FLOPs per step, where $N$ is the total number of frozen adapted-layer
parameters.  This should be compared with the usual
$6N n_{\mathrm{tok}}$ count for full finetuning
\cite{kaplan2020scaling}.
LoRA factors  contain
\[
    r(d_{\mathrm{in}}+d_{\mathrm{out}})
\]
trainable parameters and incur FLOPs of order
$n_{\mathrm{tok}}r(d_{\mathrm{in}}+d_{\mathrm{out}})$.  Since
$r\ll d_{\mathrm{in}},d_{\mathrm{out}}$, we omit these terms in the
leading-order count.


\paragraph{Optimizer.}
The \methodname{} optimizer (\Cref{alg:ours}) update runs once per optimizer step and does not
depend on $n_{\mathrm{tok}}$.  
We now count the leading operations that scale with
$d_{\mathrm{in}}$ or $d_{\mathrm{out}}$. 
The leading terms consist of forming
\[
    C_A=B^\top \bar P B,
    \qquad
    C_B=A\bar QA^\top,
\]
the preconditioned sign inputs, 
the small-side Gram matrices for the two
matrix signs, applying the accumulated Gram maps to recover the signs,
post-preconditioning the signs, and computing the diagonal metric updates
\[
    \diag(G_A^\top C_A^{-1}G_A),
    \qquad
    \diag(G_B C_B^{-1}G_B^\top).
\]
With diagonal $\bar P, \bar Q$, these operations contribute
$12r^2(d_{\mathrm{in}}+d_{\mathrm{out}})$
leading FLOPs.

The Newton--Schulz iterations themselves run in the small $r\times r$ Gram
space.  One Gram Newton--Schulz call costs $8Kr^3$ FLOPs:
$2r^3$ to form $R^2$, $2r^3$ for $Q\leftarrow MQ$, and $4r^3$ for
$R\leftarrow MRM$.  Each layer uses four such calls: two matrix signs and two
curvature inverse square roots.  Hence the small-matrix iterative work is $32Kr^3$.
Ignoring lower-order elementwise operations, diagonal scalings, spectral-norm
power iterations, and one-time re-anchoring terms, the leading optimizer cost is
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mathrm{opt}}
  =
  12r^2(d_{\mathrm{in}}+d_{\mathrm{out}})
  +
  32Kr^3 .
  \label{eq:flop-opt}
\end{equation}



\paragraph{Overhead fraction.} Dividing \eqref{eq:flop-opt} by \eqref{eq:flop-fb} gives the
per-layer optimizer FLOP overhead 
\begin{equation}
  \frac{C_{\mathrm{opt}}}{C_{\mathrm{fwd}}^{\mathrm{base}}
  +C_{\mathrm{bwd}}^{\mathrm{base}}}
  =
  \frac{
    12r^2(d_{\mathrm{in}}+d_{\mathrm{out}})
    +
    32Kr^3
  }{
    4d_{\mathrm{in}}d_{\mathrm{out}}n_{\mathrm{tok}}
  }
  =
  \frac{3r^2}{n_{\mathrm{tok}}}
  \left(
    \frac1{d_{\mathrm{in}}}
    +
    \frac1{d_{\mathrm{out}}}
  \right)
  +
  \frac{
    8Kr^3
  }{
    d_{\mathrm{in}}d_{\mathrm{out}}n_{\mathrm{tok}}
  } .
  \label{eq:flop-ratio}
\end{equation}
Thus, for fixed model dimensions, rank, and Newton--Schulz iteration count, the
optimizer FLOP fraction decays as $1/n_{\mathrm{tok}}$.
The first term is
governed by the smaller of the two layer dimensions. The second is divided by their product,
so relative to the first it weighs most on the square attention matrices, where the two
dimensions are comparable, and less on the wide feed-forward matrices. 
The whole-model overhead is an average of these per-layer fractions weighted by layer size, so
it is dominated by the large feed-forward matrices, which have the smallest fraction. 

\paragraph{Why the Gram form.} All of the dependence on $K$ sits in the $32Kr^3$ term, the
Gram Newton--Schulz. It computes the matrix sign and the curvature inverse-square-roots by
iterating on the small $r\times r$ Gram matrix rather than on the tall $d\times r$ factor
(\Cref{app:gramns}), which is about $d/r$ cheaper per iteration. Iterating on the factor
instead would make the optimizer step roughly $1.5\times$ costlier, increasing its share of
the wall (\Cref{tab:breadth}).

\section{Factor Self-Balancing}
\label{app:gauge}
By \eqref{eq:ours-rho}, \methodname{}'s step size $\rho$ depends on the two factor norms $\norm{A}_2$ and $\norm{B}_2$. Rescaling $(A,B)\mapsto(cA,c^{-1}B)$ leaves the product $BA$, and hence the model, unchanged, but changes these norms, and with them $\rho$. Among scalar rescalings $(A,B)\mapsto(cA,c^{-1}B)$ of a fixed 
factorization $BA$, $\rho$ is largest when the two factor norms are equal. An imbalanced factorization therefore takes a smaller step and follows a different trajectory. LoRA-Muon raises this concern for Spectron-style step sizes \cite{loramuon}.

In our runs this problem does not arise, because the factors balance dynamically. Starting from $B=0$, the ratio $\norm{B}_2/\norm{A}_2$ climbs and settles near $1$ (\Cref{fig:gauge}), so the factorization stays near the balanced point where $\rho$ is largest.
